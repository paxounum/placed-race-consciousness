Dear Reader,

Thank you for visiting my Placing Racial Consciousness map! There’s a lot going on in here, so let me introduce you to a few things you can find here…

Where do we learn about race?

As a white person, I was curious to ask white friends and contacts about their unfolding racial consciousness. From personal experience, I recognize that white folks are often left with the utterly incomplete and dangerously unjust understanding that “race is for other people,” when really it was a myth of “whiteness” itself which propagated the social construction of race as a way to identify peoples that fascists believed to be inferior. Such behavior, such attitudes were at the core of creating entire markets, entire infrastructures to condemn black, African, Latin, Asian, and Middle Eastern peoples to a racist political economy of less-than, poised to uplift and empower white voices, white capital, white space.

Race, as any market force, has in turn morphed our physical and social landscapes. Race has become embedded in place. This map illustrates how a small handful of participants identified with their racial consciousness in place, as a result of people, powers, and phenomena which American racial apartheid perpetuates there. But, as you will see, this is not merely a map of the United States of America. An interesting thing we see here is the mobility of these participants and what that movement meant for them. Some participants learned about race from their upbringings, some found revolutionary thoughts once they left home.

Story, Point by Point

These perspectives are mapped according to 3 unique points, what I have called the Focal Point - where one first encountered difference and was taught how to approach it, Pivot Point - where this approach towards difference, race, and behavior was challenged and perhaps changed, and a Point of Return - a homeplace of consciousness where one might return to learn more, turns to for solace, or continues to revere for wisdom and guidance. Lastly, each participant offered a place they physically or socially call Home right now.

Each entry was pinned on the map by the participant, but some locations have been abstracted for the sake of some anonymity and safety. The participants were introduced to these ideas as a way of processing their story through a mapped interview form, and that data was cleaned and prepared to be uploaded to this site.

You will find, as you click through each person’s story that they answered prompts about Focal, Pivot, and Return points with openness and reflection. That is how I invite you to read their stories: with openness and reflection. Some of these stories may feel awkward, some familiar, some might not sit well with you. This is the nature of life in process, but this is also the nature of the incongruent language we have been given to talk about difference in America.

Towards Decentering Whiteness

A helpful reflection and guide to move past this categorization of race into a more justly collaborative, multiracial society was written by Jeff Hitchcock and Charley Flint called “Decentering Whiteness.”

In their work, they identify how white folks often unconsciously hold the center (and thus force others to the margins), by presuming whiteness as “the standard,” or their general behaviors as “morally correct” and thus not needing challenge, or “comfortable” and “secure.” This sensory, felt normalcy by white folks leaves them stead in their complicity of larger marginalizing projects, such as Jim Crow tactics, spatial and social segregation, and unequal access to common resources such as food, water, and shelter.

It is against this dangerous ignorance, complicity, and violence that I offer the personal reflections on this map. You may ask - wait, aren’t you centering whiteness by talking only to white folks about race? Well, you’re not wrong. This project seeks to be informed by its limitations. At first, I had goals to collect a broad assortment of participants, but then, as I searched my Self, I realized that the questions I was reflecting on personally might differ than those I would need to pose to respect the lives and stories of others. Additionally, as I “tried out my process,” I wanted to be sensitive to BIPOC participants who have had plenty of processes “tried out” on them before. So, I decided to do my test run on white people this time around.

Could I use whiteness as a lens to highlight difference? To frame stories of learning and change? That success is up to you, as a reader of this map, to decide. Does this map take you anywhere helpful?

I’d invite you to join me in this process of moving towards what Hitchcock and Flint call a multiracial society, where we give from our centers, not take and hoard from them, co-creating commonly held, culturally literate futures.

Map Characteristics: How to Read these Stories

My Story + Pick a Story

My story is a great place to start! Here, I dig into some of the places I saw race and “place” informed in my life. I use the Focal/Pivot/Return points in a more extensive sense across a wider range of spaces and times than what you’ll find on the map.

Then, check out each participant’s story by picking it from the dropdown menu. The map will be simplified to just the storyteller’s points, and one or two regions that relate to this. You may click through the different Points of each story, and then you can return to see the complexity of them all together.

Identity Regions

As we are inquiring about how race relates to place, I was also curious about how these identified places relate to wider spaces and systems. If race has been a tool for dividing space, such as redlining (See the Mapping Inequality map here. ) or senses of safety and belonging (See the Greenbook Map here. ), how do our individual identities and stories interact with larger systems - natural and human-made?

In order to set up these questions, I have included five layers of a key systems that were mentioned by our storytellers: the Rust Belt, the Appalachian Mountains, the Mississippi River Basin Watershed (huge, isn’t it!?), Native Land as categorized by US government-recognized reservations (understandably limited), and Crude Oil pipelines. These “systems” are decidedly heterogeneous in sources and form - from river basins formed by the earth, to piping and industrial zones built to exploit the earth. But, by including these regions, I invite the readers to examine how these might interact with the exploitative nature of race, difference, and segregation tactics - massive systems which construe our economies and ecologies to benefit the few.

So many more regions could be included here - agriculture zones, population centers and demographics, pollution and production spaces, etc - but these are a start. What other systems, regions, and forces are you familiar with in the places we’ve mapped together? How might they inform locals’ identities, livelihoods, futures?
